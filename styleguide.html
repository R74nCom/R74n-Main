Styleguide

Official Styleguide for R74n projects and lists canonical forms of many words and phrases.

This standard is highly specialized for short and informative web content. Feel free to use it in your own writing, or adapt it!

Effective from 28 October 2026. Updated frequently.

Tone

Write concisely, clearly, and semi-informally, while still giving an authoritative impression on the topic.

Provide examples where necessary in order to convey the desired information. Keep humor to a minimum on informative webpages.

Spelling & capitalization

General

  • For English text, American spelling is preferred over British. For proper nouns, spelling should be the exact same as the official one (the Labour Party).
    • color, not colour
    • realize, not realise
    • meter, not metre
    • program, not programme
    • gray, not grey
    • defense, not defence
    • cancelled, not canceled (An exception)
    • judgement, not judgment (Another exception)
    • meow, not miaow
    • airplane, not aeroplane
    • aluminum, not aluminium
    • fetus, not foetus
    • jail, not gaol
  • In titles, determiners (the) and prepositions (of) are not capitalized, except as the first word. (The United States of America)
  • Pages should not start with "This is a ..." or anything similar. Instead, jump straight to the point (Collection of ...).
  • Text content on the main R74n website is written in first-person-plural perspective, meaning R74n is referred to by "we", "our", and "us".
  • In a series of gendered forms, the order should follow: Gender neutral, feminine, masculine.
  • Non-title headers should be capitalized with sentence case (Recent changes).
  • Text content generally uses family-friendly words and phrases to refer to sensitive subjects.
  • If a person's pronouns are not explicitly mentioned in their public profile(s), use they/them/their/themself.
  • Job titles and positions, apart from proper nouns, are generally lowercase (... the Treasury secretary), except when immediately preceding names (President Roosevelt).
  • In scientific/Latin names of species (Xenopus laevis), the first word is capitalized.
  • Contractions (they'll) are encouraged in order to keep a friendly tone.

Punctuation

hyphen

  • Used to form compound words (berry-flavored), including in segments (oil- and water-based).
  • Used to separate phrases in a sentence (In Sandboxels - our falling-sand simulator - players have ...). Traditionally, the now-deprecated em dash had served this purpose.
  • Used as bullet points in plain-text contexts, where HTML lists aren't possible.
  • Used to abruptly end a sentence with style, as if interrupted.
  • Used to convert phrases into adjectives (The first-floor restroom).
  • Used to denote prefixes (semi-), infixes (-flippin'-), and suffixes (-able).
  • In titles, only the first letter of hyphenated are capitalized (Follow-up), except for cases where proper nouns are included (Non-American).

em dash

  • Due to associations with generative AI, em dashes are being deprecated across R74n. Hyphens are now used instead.

en dash

  • Used to separate ends of timespans (July–November) or other numerical ranges (50–100).

quotation mark

  • Double quotes are used for quotations.
  • Straight quotation marks are used ("...") instead of curled variants (“...”) inserted automatically by some devices.
  • Punctuation is placed after a quotation mark when terminating a sentence (She said "I'm not sure".).
  • Commas are placed after a quotation mark when continuing a list (Kittens, "puppers", and more).

apostrophe

  • Straight apostrophes are used for both possession ('), contraction, and quotation ('...'), instead of curled variants (‘...’) inserted automatically by some devices.
  • Possessive forms depend on the word being singular or plural, and not what it ends with. 's is always included after singular names.
    • Ms. Adams's dog
    • the Adams' dog
    • the United States's economy (United States is singular; Better to say "the American economy")
    • the IRS's employees
  • Single quotes are used for quotations within quotations.

ellipsis

  • Ellipses are composed of three separate period characters (...), instead of the combined single character (…), with no spaces in between them.

comma

  • Commas separate all items of a list, including the last two (i.e. Oxford/Serial commas are included).
    • She likes cats, dogs, and birds.
    • He'll eat apples, oranges, or bananas.
  • "If you do x or y, you will z."
  • "Enter the x into the y, and you will z."
  • "... x, y, and more."

ampersand

  • Ampersands (&) are preferred for use in titles, while "and" should be used in text content.
  • Spaces do not appose ampersands in single constructions, such as in Q&A or PB&J.

parenthesis

  • The first word in parentheses is capitalized, as if the content is its own sentence.
  • Words or phrases separated by forward slashes or en dashes are capitalized like so:
    • (Mobile/Cellular devices)
    • (On mobile/cellular devices)
    • (On mobile/On cellular)
    • (Five–Ten items)
    • (Between five–ten items)
  • Parenthetical plurals should be used sparingly, with the rest of the sentence assuming the plural, like so:
    • They pet dog(s).
    • They pack box(es).
    • They receive candy(s). (Instead of -ies to preserve searchability)
    • The dog(s) are playing on their own.
  • Lone periods do not precede a closing parenthesis. Other punctuation can be included at the end for emphasis, such as ellipses (...), exclamation marks (!), or question marks (?).
  • Parentheses come after semicolons and commas in the order of operations - meaning that they only apply up to the nearest preceding semicolon or comma, if any.
    • They pet the cats, dogs, and lizards (reluctantly).
    • They (reluctantly) pet the cats, dogs, and lizards.
    • They pet the cats, dogs (reluctantly), and lizards.

numeral

  • Small numbers (Zero–Twelve) are spelled out when in text content, while all numbers should be in numerical form when in dates, times, tables, or other data formats.
    • zero, one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, and twelve.
    • negative one, negative two, negative three, ...
  • Ordinal numbers follow the same rule. Alongside text content, small numbers are spelled out (First–Twelfth), otherwise they are written numerically (1st, 2nd, 3rd, 20th).
  • Small ordinal numbers (First–Twelfth) alongside text content are written out (First, second, third, fourth). When alone, they are written numerically (1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th).
  • Ordered lists have their items numbered followed by a period (1. 2. 3.).
  • Decimal numbers less than one include a leading zero (0.125).
  • Avoid abbreviating thousands (1,000, 2,000) below 100,000 to avoid confusion with resolutions (4K). If abbreviation is necessary, capitalize it (1K, 2K). Abbreviate hundred thousands (100K), millions (1M), billions (1B), and, when context is clear, trillions (1T) and quadrillions (1Q).
  • Unit abbreviations should be separated from the number by a space, like so:
    • 10 km
    • 200 ft
    • 3,000 m
    • 5 GB
    • 40 Gbit
    • 2 Gbps
    • 120 km/h
    • 80 mph
    • 30 FPS
  • Currency symbols are written before (except when under 1 unit) the number, without a space, in the native currency of the subject, like so:
    • $10
    • 50¢
    • £10
    • 50p

colon

  • The first word following a colon is capitalized, as if starting a new sentence.

semicolon

  • Used to separate clauses which could make sense as their own sentences.
  • The first word following a semicolon is capitalized, as if starting a new sentence.

bullet point

  • If HTML is possible, the bullet character (•) is used by inserting an <li> tag, which should apply it automatically with CSS. Otherwise, use a hyphen (-).
  • The first word of a bullet or numbered list item is capitalized, as if starting a new sentence.

less- and greater-than sign

  • When comparing two values, the sign is surrounded by spaces (x ≤ 10).
  • When comparing one value, the sign is directly attached to the number (>10 items).

emoji

  • Unless contextually appropriate, emoji are preferred to omit skin tone (Default yellow) and gender.

When a sentence ends with a hyperlink, punctuation should be placed after the hyperlink instead of within it, like this.

Font style

bold

  • Used to improve readability by highlighting important concepts in a sentence.

italics

  • Used to add emphasis, i.e. when specifying something that may be overlooked (Upload the save file, not a screenshot).
  • When introducing the subject of the page or section, use the <dfn> tag.
  • Used to highlight a newly introduced subject, especially titles of media (Sandboxels is a game. You can do a lot in Sandboxels ...).
  • Scientific/Latin names of species (Felis catus) are italicized.

Interface

hyperlink

  • Unintended tracking codes and all other unnecessary parameters must be removed from copy-and-pasted URLs.
  • Only use shortened URLs when displayed as plain text. Hyperlinks should include the full URL, unless shortened for demonstrative purposes.

Graphics

Maps

Where possible, the UN-adopted Equal Earth projection should be used to display the entire Earth instead of the Mercator projection.

Color

Where possible, skin tones should follow or be based on the Monk Skin Tone (MST) Scale.
